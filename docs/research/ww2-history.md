## "The guns pointed the wrong way": the accurate version

Singapore's biggest coastal guns were five 15-inch guns: three at Johore Battery (Changi) and two at
Buona Vista. **Three of the five could turn a full 360°**, and they did fire north and west at the
Japanese. The two Johore Battery guns that could turn fired 194 rounds, starting on 5 and 7 Feb against
targets in Johor. The real problem was **ammunition and trajectory**. The guns had mostly
**armour-piercing shells** meant for warships. These bury themselves before exploding, so they do little
against troops, and the guns' flat trajectory suited targets at sea, not over land. The guns did what they were
built for: they deterred an attack from the sea. Singapore fell because of weak defences on the
northern shore, the loss of air and naval cover (especially the sinking of *Prince of Wales* and *Repulse*),
and the speed of the Japanese advance and British command failures. Churchill's memoirs helped spread
the myth. Sources: [Hillsdale Churchill Project][guns], [NLB Johore Battery][nlb-jb],
[NAS Johore Battery marker text][nas-jb].

## Civilians during raids

Singapore's ground is low-lying with a high water table, so there were few deep underground shelters.
People took cover in **dug trenches** lined with planks and sandbags, **under staircases and tables**,
in sandbagged ground floors, and in the few purpose-built shelters. For example, the SIT flats at Tiong Bahru
had a shelter for almost 2,000 people, and an IWM photo shows civilians in the Guan Chuan Street shelter.
Shophouse **five-foot ways** gave some cover from falling debris, and people crowded along them
between shelters. The government advised against evacuating the city. About 6,000 men and women
from every community volunteered for civil defence: **ARP wardens**, the Medical Auxiliary Service,
the fire service and the Observer Corps. Wardens patrolled during alerts, enforced the blackout and sent
people to shelter. Families stockpiled rice and tinned food, especially sardines. Sources:
[BiblioAsia: Preparing for War][ba-prep], [Tiong Bahru Air Raid Shelter][tb-shelter].

A **sandbagged shelter** in a Chinatown shophouse street, with taped windows, is accurate. The "no failure/death"
design choice is fine as long as the scene still carries danger. The text has a shophouse down the street
hit during the run.

## Newspapers and "Fortress Singapore" rhetoric

- Before the war, the press called Singapore the "Gibraltar of the East", an "impregnable" fortress.
  For example, a *Malaya Tribune* article in Feb 1941 said Malaya was becoming "one of the most
  impregnable fortresses in the Far East" ([Malaya Tribune, 20 Feb 1941][mt-1941]).
- **"Singapore Must Stand; It SHALL Stand"**, a message from the Governor, Sir Shenton Thomas,
  appeared in *The Straits Times* on 15 Feb 1942, the paper's last issue before the surrender, cut down to a
  single sheet ([NewspaperSG, 15 Feb 1942][st-0215]). Siti's line, "Singapore will stand!", paraphrases it. Her
  scene is set on 12 Feb, so do not show the exact headline as a prop with an earlier date.
- **Chinese press:** *Nanyang Siang Pau* (founded 1923 by Tan Kah Kee) and *Sin Chew Jit Poh* (1929,
  Aw Boon Haw) had covered Japan's war in China for years. Both **stopped printing a few days before
  15 Feb 1942**. The Japanese took over their presses (the *Syonan Jit Poh* / *Zhaonan Ribao* from
  21 Feb). Both papers returned in September 1945 ([NLB Nanyang Siang Pau][nlb-nysp]).
- **Malay press:** *Utusan Melayu* was founded in Singapore on 29 May 1939. It was the first Malay paper wholly
  owned and run by Malays, and **Yusof Ishak**, later Singapore's first President, was one of its founders.
  During the occupation it was merged into *Berita Malai* ([NLB Utusan Melayu][nlb-um]).
- Under the occupation the English paper became the *Syonan Times / Syonan Shimbun* (from 20 Feb 1942).

## Was 1942 Chinatown multi-ethnic enough for this cast?

Yes. Chinatown was mostly Chinese (Hokkien, Cantonese, Teochew, Hakka, Hainanese), but not only
Chinese. Telok Ayer Street has the Chulia (Tamil Muslim) **Al-Abrar Mosque** and **Nagore Dargah** next to
**Thian Hock Keng**. South Bridge Road has the Hindu **Sri Mariamman Temple** and the Chulia **Jamae Mosque**. Indian
traders and moneylenders lived around South Bridge Road, Upper Cross Street, Market and Chulia Streets.
Malay neighbourhoods were a short walk away (Kampong Bahru, Telok Blangah, Tanjong Pagar), and Malay
newspapers were sold across the town ([Harmony in Diversity Gallery][hidg],
[Remember Singapore: Telok Ayer][ra-telok]).

- **Indian ARP warden**: plausible. Volunteers came from every community.
- **Chinese kopitiam run by an elderly woman**: plausible. Most kopitiam were run by Hainanese or Foochow
  men, so she is more likely a widow who took over the shop.
- **Malay girl and Malay older man on the street**: plausible as passers-by or workers (see the
  job notes above).
- Ah Ma speaks some Malay and Hokkien, and Siti and Hassan understand bazaar Malay. Everyday Malay
  was the common language across communities, so "adik", "terima kasih", "aiyah/aiyoh" and "lah" fit.

## How the text handles the Sook Ching

The epilogue shows the Sook Ching only through what is missing. Boon's father answers the order to report
to **Hong Lim Green** and does not come back. Ah Ma pours his kopi every morning. This is
representative. BiblioAsia's family history says few Singapore Chinese families did not lose one or
more relatives. Many families never learned what happened: no body, no record, "weeks, then months,
then years" of waiting ([BiblioAsia: The Sook Ching][ba-sc]). The chapter never shows or describes violence.

## Night search and rumours beats (14–15 Feb 1942)

These beats replace the old shelter-talk beat in `ww2-text.js` (`shelterTransition`, `blackout`, `rumours`).

- **Shelling of the city, night of 14 Feb:** accurate. In the last days, about a million civilians were crowded
  into the shrinking British-held area around the city. Japanese artillery on the Bukit Timah heights and
  aircraft hit it day and night, fires burned across town, and hospitals overflowed. About 7,000 civilians
  died in the fighting on the island, in addition to earlier raids. Shells whistling in, blackout and burning
  streets are all fair to show. Stragglers and soldiers who had lost their units were in the streets, which is where
  the "Soldier" fragment comes from. Sources: [Anzac Portal: Fall of Singapore][anzac],
  [NLB Battle of Singapore][nlb-bos], [NAS timeline][nas-tl].
- **"Officers with a white flag going up Bukit Timah Road":** true. On 15 Feb a British party went out to the
  Japanese lines under a Union Flag and a white flag: Brigadier Newbigging, Colonial Secretary Hugh Fraser and
  Major Cyril Wild as interpreter. Percival himself reached the Ford Factory at about 5.15 pm, signed
  at 6.10 pm, and the ceasefire was at 8.30 pm. Sources: [The Cove: Leadership in the Malayan Campaign][cove],
  [NAS timeline][nas-tl].
- **"American ships are coming" and "the British will fight street by street":** both are invented, but they are the
  kind of rumour people spread. Hopes of Allied relief convoys were common. Churchill had ordered a fight to the end,
  and street fighting in the city was one of the fears behind Percival's decision.
- **The faraway wireless:** paraphrase only. See the radio note in the corrections table.
- **Headline:** **"Singapore Must Stand; It SHALL Stand"**, a message from H.E. the Governor (Sir Shenton Thomas),
  in the *Straits Times* issue dated Sunday 15 Feb 1942. It is filed on NewspaperSG as `straitstimes19420215`
  and is often called the *Sunday Times* edition. This is the paper's widely cited last issue before the
  occupation ([NewspaperSG][st-0215]). The wording and date are confirmed from NewspaperSG's indexed
  text. I couldn't view the page scan (it loads with JavaScript), so its exact typography and placement
  are unconfirmed. If the puzzle shows it as a prop, keep the wording exactly and use a generic period masthead.
