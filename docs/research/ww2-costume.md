# Costume research — Singapore, c. 1940–1942 (Chapter 1 NPCs) + present-day Mr. Boon

Purpose: give the Chapter 1 NPCs (Siti, Mr. Rajan, Ah Ma, Ah Boon, Pak Hassan) everyday clothing that
fits the period, drawn with respect and warmth, and never as a caricature. The models are stylised
plush toys, so we keep the silhouette, colour and one or two telling details, not every seam.

Notes on confidence: **[S]** means a cited source states it directly. **[I]** means a design
inference from the sources plus general period photographs. Mark anything new the same way.

---

## 1. Elderly Chinese woman — samfu (Ah Ma)

- **[S]** Samfu is the Cantonese name for the everyday two-piece outfit of a *sam* (blouse) and
  *fu* (trousers). It was common among Chinese in South China, Hong Kong and Singapore until the
  mid-20th century. It let working women move freely while keeping them modestly covered.
  - NLB Infopedia, "Samfu": https://eresources.nlb.gov.sg/infopedia/articles/SIP_2013-09-27_175007.html
- **[S]** The classic amah look was a **white blouse with ankle-length black trousers and black
  slippers** (the "black-and-white" samfu). Samsui women wore **dark-blue or black** samfu because
  the dark colours hide stains.
  - BiblioAsia, "A Lifetime of Labour: Cantonese Amahs in Singapore" (2017):
    https://biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg/vol-13/issue-3/oct-dec-2017/lifetime-of-labour/
  - NLB Infopedia, "Samsui women": https://eresources.nlb.gov.sg/infopedia/articles/SIP_795_2005-01-18.html
  - NLB Infopedia, "Majie": https://eresources.nlb.gov.sg/infopedia/articles/SIP_2013-11-29_160454.html
- **[I]** Construction to show: a mandarin-style stand collar, a **side-fastening (diagonal) front
  opening with cloth "frog" knot buttons**, wide elbow-to-wrist sleeves, and a loose hip-length top.
  Older women wore their hair pulled back tightly into a **low bun at the nape** (often with a hairpin).
  Plain cotton; light colours (white, pale blue, pale grey) for the top.
- **Design choice:** a **pale blue-grey samfu top** with darker blue frog buttons, **black
  trousers**, black cloth slippers, a grey bun with a jade/wood pin, and an optional off-white cloth
  apron for the kopitiam (it reads as her trade, not a costume). A slight forward stoop, no
  exaggerated wrinkles: warm cheeks, kind eyes.

## 2. Young Malay girl — baju kurung (Siti, ~12)

- **[S]** The baju kurung (loosely "concealing dress") is a knee-length blouse worn over a long
  skirt. It is mainly associated with the Malay/Muslim community of Singapore and worn both
  formally and casually.
  - NLB, "Baju kurong": https://www.nlb.gov.sg/main/article-detail?cmsuuid=8c5d2bc8-30b4-48e9-8a41-5f5794cae66b
  - Roots.gov.sg, "Making and Wearing of Baju Kurung": https://www.roots.gov.sg/ich-landing/ich/making-and-wearing-of-baju-kurung
- **[S]** Before the 1950s, Malays favoured loose clothing suited to the humid weather. The baju
  kurung was the traditional choice for both men and women; women paired the knee-length top with a
  matching skirt.
  - BiblioAsia, "The Way We Were: Fashion Through the Decades" (2017):
    https://biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg/vol-13/issue-3/oct-dec-2017/fashionthroughdecades/
- **[S]** The **Teluk Belanga** cut (a round neck with no collar and a small slit finished with
  *tulang belut* stitching) takes its name from the Singapore district that was once Johor's seat. It
  became the standard baju kurung style. The top's length **"stayed just below the knee until the
  early 1940s."** The large *kain kelubung* head-wrap was used up to the 1930s, and the *selendang*
  (a lighter shawl) replaced it from the 1950s.
  - Wikipedia, "Baju Kurung" (it cites the Malaysian national dress literature): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baju_Kurung
  - JKKN Malaysia culture map, "Baju Kurung Teluk Belanga": https://pemetaanbudaya.jkkn.gov.my/senibudaya/detail/1350
- **[I]** A 12-year-old working girl in 1942 would most likely wear a **plain or small-print cotton
  baju kurung**, sometimes with a skirt of a different colour or a batik sarong, and go bareheaded or
  with a light scarf over her shoulders. Hair: **one or two long plaits**, or tied back. The
  full-coverage tudung is a later norm, so we don't model it for 1942. Footwear: simple sandals or
  bare feet.
- **Design choice (built):** a **soft green Teluk Belanga baju kurung** that reaches below the
  knee, a **batik-brown skirt** with a simple motif band, dark hair parted in the middle into two
  plaits tied with red ribbons, brown sandals, and a cross-body **canvas bag of
  newspapers** (period papers: *The Straits Times*, *Utusan Melayu*, *Nanyang Siang Pau*).

## 3. Malay man's work clothes and headwear (Pak Hassan)

- **[S]** Basic Malay dress was the *kain* (cloth wrap or sarong), the *baju* (shirt) and the
  *destar* (headcloth). The *tengkolok/destar* belonged to nobility, while the common people used the
  simpler tied **semutar** headcloth. The Teluk Belanga baju had three-quarter sleeves to make daily
  work easier.
  - Wikipedia, "Baju Melayu": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baju_Melayu
  - Wikipedia, "Malaysian cultural outfits": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaysian_cultural_outfits
- **[S]** The **songkok** is a brimless felt or velvet cap, usually black, worn by Malay Muslim
  men. In the colonial period it was also part of the Malay Regiment's uniform.
  - Roots.gov.sg, "Making and Wearing of Songkok": https://www.roots.gov.sg/ich-landing/ich/making-and-wearing-of-songkok
  - Wikipedia, "Songkok": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Songkok
- **[I]** For heavy labour: a **singlet or a loose collarless shirt with sleeves rolled up**, a
  **checked kain pelikat sarong hitched up** (or short trousers under it), and a **tied headcloth
  (semutar)** for sweat and sun. On a pulling job the songkok is less practical, so a headcloth fits
  better; a songkok can be kept for the 1965 / off-duty version.

## 4. Rickshaw pullers (Pak Hassan's trade)

- **[S]** Rickshaws were introduced to Singapore in 1880 and abolished on 1 May 1947. Pullers
  "often wore little else than blue denims", went barefoot or wore **sandals cut from car tyres**,
  and often wore a **wide conical hat**.
  - NLB Infopedia, "Rickshaw": https://eresources.nlb.gov.sg/infopedia/articles/SIP_947_2005-01-25.html
  - National Archives of Singapore, "Rickshaw Pullers and Trishaw Riders": https://www.nas.gov.sg/archivesonline/trades_labourtransport3
- **[S]** Most pullers were **Chinese immigrants** (Hokchia/Hockchew and Henghua from Fujian).
  Malays and Indians worked more often as *gharry* (horse-cab) drivers.
  - J. F. Warren, *Rickshaw Coolie: A People's History of Singapore 1880–1940*:
    https://books.google.com/books/about/Rickshaw_Coolie.html?id=wVyqbmHS2zQC
  - J. F. Warren, "The Singapore Rickshaw Pullers" (JSEAS):
    https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-southeast-asian-studies/article/abs/singapore-rickshaw-pullers-the-social-organization-of-a-coolie-occupation-18801940/7CF66F0C805E92F3EA831AD058B2AC2F
- **DECISION (story correction):** because 1942 pullers were almost all Chinese, **Pak Hassan is
  NOT a rickshaw puller. He is a Malay street hawker (satay seller).** **[S]** Itinerant hawkers
  carried their stoves and wares on a shoulder pole (*kandar*) and were a common street sight in
  colonial Singapore. Satay is a long-established Malay/Javanese hawker dish. **[I]** Note: the
  famous Beach Road "Satay Club" only dates from the late 1940s, so don't cite it for 1942.
  - NLB Infopedia, "Satay": https://eresources.nlb.gov.sg/infopedia/articles/SIP_888_2005-01-10.html
  - NLB, "Travelling hawkers": https://www.nlb.gov.sg/main/article-detail?cmsuuid=ede5738c-465b-49ce-9b3f-4001ad6826e5
  - NLB Infopedia, "Satay Club" (late-1940s origin): https://eresources.nlb.gov.sg/infopedia/articles/SIP_1027_2006-08-12.html
- **Design choice (built):** a collarless **Teluk Belanga-style work baju** in faded indigo with
  a round neck, *tulang belut*-style trim and **rolled sleeves**. Over it, a **green-and-brown checked
  kain pelikat** to mid-shin, a soft **maroon cloth sash** at the waist, a black **songkok**, brown
  sandals, a neat moustache, and a lean, kind face. He still has the twisted ankle, so the Limp clip
  favours the right leg. A folded headcloth (*semutar*) is supported in the script
  (`headwear=dict(type='headcloth')`) if the writers prefer it.

## 5. Chinese boy (Ah Boon, ~7)

- **[I]** Period street photographs (NAS PictureSG; see BiblioAsia's PictureSG feature) show
  working-class boys in **white or grey cotton singlets** with **dark shorts**, often barefoot or in
  wooden clogs or rubber slippers. Hair was cropped short or shaved for heat and hygiene.
  - BiblioAsia, "Picturing Singapore: A Visual History": https://biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg/all-sections/vol-16-issue-2-jul-sep-2020-picturessg/
  - NLB PictureSG catalogue: https://eresources.nlb.gov.sg/pictures/
- **Design choice:** a **white singlet** (slightly off-white), **navy shorts**, bare feet, a short
  crop with a small cowlick, round cheeks and a big grin. He's curious and sweet, and never shown as
  a street urchin.

## 6. Singapore ARP / Passive Defence warden (Mr. Rajan)

- **[S]** The Air Raid Precautions department was set up in Singapore in 1938. By November 1939 it
  had about **3,500 wardens**, who were mobilised for the first raid on **8 December 1941**. Wardens
  patrolled streets, got people into shelters and enforced the blackout. About 6,000 men and women
  volunteered across the civil-defence services (ARP, Medical Auxiliary Service, fire squads, and
  others). Veterans recall burying their "uniform, helmet" when Singapore fell.
  - BiblioAsia, "In Their Own Voices: Preparing for War in Singapore" (2023):
    https://biblioasia.nlb.gov.sg/vol-18/issue-4/jan-mar-2023/preparing-war-singapore/
  - NLB Infopedia, "First air raid on Singapore": https://eresources.nlb.gov.sg/infopedia/articles/SIP_7_2005-01-25.html
- **[S]** A Straits Settlements *Air Raid and Bombardment Precautions Department* badge survives
  in the national collection.
  - Roots.gov.sg: https://www.roots.gov.sg/Collection-Landing/listing/1189245
- **[S]** Photographs from 1941–42 show Singapore ARP wardens drilling with incendiary bombs at
  Raffles Place.
  - Library of Congress, "Singapore's A.R.P. …": https://www.loc.gov/item/2013650311 and https://www.loc.gov/resource/ds.04815
- **[S]** Standard British-pattern kit: the **Mk II "Brodie" steel helmet**, a shallow bowl with a
  wide brim and a webbing chin strap. Warden helmets were **black with a white "W"** (seniors had a
  white helmet with a black W and stripes). Wardens also wore an **armband**, an ARP lapel badge, a
  **police whistle**, a torch, a first-aid haversack and a **gas-mask (respirator) bag**.
  - Wikipedia, "Brodie helmet": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brodie_helmet
  - WW2 Civil Defence, helmets & markings: https://www.ww2civildefence.co.uk/home-front-helmets.html
  - WW2 Civil Defence, uniforms: https://www.ww2civildefence.co.uk/uniforms.html
  - 1900s.org.uk, "ARP wardens": https://www.1900s.org.uk/1940s-arp.htm
  - Australian War Memorial, ARP "Warden" armband: https://www.awm.gov.au/collection/C1104222
  - National Army Museum, ARP warden's helmet c.1940: https://collection.nam.ac.uk/detail.php?acc=1998-12-170-1
- **[I]** In the tropics, volunteers often wore **khaki drill** (a shirt and shorts or trousers)
  instead of British dark-blue serge. Many simply wore their own clothes with the helmet and
  armband. Indians (including Sikhs) volunteered in Singapore's civil-defence and volunteer units.
- **Design choice:** a **khaki drill shirt** with two breast pockets, **khaki trousers**, brown
  shoes, a **dark Brodie helmet with a white "W"**, a **navy armband marked "ARP"** on the left
  arm, a **silver whistle on a white lanyard** clipped to the left pocket, a small
  **khaki respirator bag** on a cross strap, and a neat moustache. He looks upright, calm and
  kindly, a neighbourhood figure people trust.

## 7. Soldier who lost his unit, 15 Feb 1942 (npc-soldier)

- **[S]** The Malay Regiment (formed 1933) fought at **Pasir Panjang / Bukit Chandu on 13–14 Feb
  1942**, the ridge about 4–5 km west of Chinatown. Survivors were scattered as the line fell back
  toward the town.
  - NLB Infopedia, "Malay Regiment": https://eresources.nlb.gov.sg/infopedia/articles/SIP_1748_2011-01-07.html
  - Wikipedia, "Battle of Pasir Panjang": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Pasir_Panjang
  - Wikipedia, "Adnan Saidi": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adnan_Saidi
- **[S]** Indian Army troops were the largest non-British contingent. About 45,000 Indian POWs
  assembled at Farrer Park on 17 Feb 1942.
  - Wikipedia, "Farrer Park address": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farrer_Park_address
  - IWM, "Why did Singapore fall?": https://www.iwm.org.uk/history/why-did-singapore-fall
- **Choice:** a **Malay Regiment private** separated after Bukit Chandu. He is the most plausible
  *local* straggler on a Chinatown street on 15 Feb, and he ties into a story Singapore
  commemorates (Reflections at Bukit Chandu). An Indian Army sepoy is statistically as plausible; the
  config can switch to one by changing skin and face values. For Sikh soldiers, use a turban
  instead of the helmet.
- **[I]** Kit: **khaki drill shirt** (sleeves rolled) and knee-length **KD shorts**, **hose
  tops + puttees**, ammunition **boots**, **1937-pattern webbing** (belt, two basic pouches, braces),
  a water bottle, and a plain **Mk II steel helmet** in drab (**no "W"/ARP markings**). The Malay
  Regiment's slouch hat was its parade and identity headwear; steel helmets were worn in action.
  - Wikipedia, "Uniforms of the Malaysian Army": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniforms_of_the_Malaysian_Army
- **Design:** he's tired but dignified. Upper lids are half-lowered, brows worried, the stoop is
  slight, the uniform lightly soiled, and he carries **no weapon**.

## 8. Mr. Boon at 91, present day (npc-oldboon)

- He shares young Boon's "face DNA": the same round head proportions, eye spacing, brow arch and
  wide grin. Age shows through a thinning white crown with combed-over wisps, gentle smile lines,
  round wire glasses and a slight stoop.
- **[I]** He wears the everyday outfit of an older Singaporean "uncle" at an HDB void deck: a
  **pinstriped short-sleeved button shirt**, untucked, with one chest pocket, **grey slacks**,
  **blue rubber slippers**, and a **wooden walking cane** (separate node `Cane`, in his left hand).

- Across the crowd, palettes, head shapes, heights (1.54–1.74 m), headwear and hair are all
  varied, so a group of 6–8 reads as different people rather than recolours.

## Respectful-design checklist (used when reviewing the renders)
- Skin tones come from natural, varied values. Facial features are the same simple plush grammar
  for everyone (dot eyes, soft brows, small mouth); no ethnic features are exaggerated.
- Clothing is clean and cared for even when it's work wear. Working people look capable, not poor.
- No slogans, flags or insignia on the NPCs beyond the historical "W"/"ARP" warden markings.
- Children are drawn as cheerful and curious, never as waifs.
